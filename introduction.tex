\section{Introduction}
Most distributed systems implementing cryptocurrency schemes~\cite{bitcoin} and smart contract~\cite{smartcontracts,wood} have been built on the assumption that it is necessary to achieve consensus on \emph{total order} of all users'  transactions. This belief sparked a rich line of work on permissionless blockchain protocols~\cite{EC:GarKiaLeo15,C:KRDO17,EC:DGKR18,EC:PasShi18,algorand} and more traditional consensus protocols~\cite{CCS:MXCSS16,PODC:YMRGA19,SP:AMNRY20,sbft,narwhal} aimed at instantiating large scale high-throughput public ledgers that maintain an (eventually) immutable global order of transactions while quickly registering honest parties' transactions. However, as much as these protocols have evolved in recent years, the need to maintain total order among all transactions in the ledger introduces fundamental constraints to their efficiency~\cite{antoniadis_et_al:LIPIcs.DISC.2018.7,FOCS:BerGarPer89,10.1007/978-3-319-39028-4_9}. Moreover, the fact that leaders get to choose what data to register in the ledger allows these leaders to unfairly order transactions~\cite{C:KZGJ20} or even momentarily censor a transaction. While solutions to these censorship~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-10185} and order fairness~\cite{CCS:KDLJK23,fairpos} problems have been suggested, they face fundamental limits~\cite{EC:KiaLeoShe24} due to aiming for full consensus guarantees.
\pagebreak

A recent line of work~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1909-10926, DBLP:conf/dsn/CollinsGKKMPPST20,DBLP:conf/aft/BaudetDS20,DBLP:journals/dc/GuerraouiKMPS22} established that consensus on a total order of transactions is actually not necessary for digital asset transfer schemes (\textit{e.g.} cryptocurrencies). The main insight in these works is that a ledger providing a \emph{partial order} of transactions is sufficient for implementing such schemes. More precisely, the ledger only needs to ensuring that, if transactions $\tx_1$ and $\tx_2$ are created by the same party, then every party outputs them in the same order. While this observation allows for constructing highly efficient protocols, previous works focus on the specific case of asset transfer and do not handle censorship. 

In this work, we investigate how to further relax consensus guarantees in order to construct distributed systems with better liveness and censorship resistance. As a motivating example, we consider distributed auctions~\cite{trustee,seal,ACNS:DavGenPou22,CCS:TAFWBM23,chitra_et_al:LIPIcs.AFT.2024.19,cryptoeprint:2024/1011}, where bids are usually registered on a public ledger.  In such auctions, parties post their bids (either sealed or open) on the public ledger before a deadline, after which a protocol is executed to determine the winning bid (\textit{e.g.} the highest bid). Notice that there is no need for the bids in an auction to be ordered, as long as parties agree on the set of bids corresponding to a given protocol execution. Hence, in this application, even the weaker guarantee of partial order among transactions from the same user is an overkill, if the leaders executing the public ledger may censor bids from certain parties. In this context, we ask the following question:

\begin{center}
	\emph{What are the weakest transaction ordering guarantees sufficient for building distributed systems with optimal latency and censorship resistance?}
\end{center}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
We answer this question by introducing the notion of \podtm, which allows for parties to read and write transactions associated with a \emph{timestamp}. Rather than agreeing on a transaction's timestamp, each party in a \podtm reads different timestamps that may later change, but are guaranteed to stay within certain limits.  This means that we may be able to order some transactions written at far enough points in time, while other transactions written around the same time may appear concurrent during the entire execution. A \podtm has a  \emph{past-perfection} property, which guarantees that, after a certain round, no honest party will read a transaction associated to a timestamp earlier than that round. A \podtm also guarantees that, within a bounded delay, written transactions can be read by all honest parties (preventing censorship) and every round becomes \emph{past-perfect}.  

We construct \podtm-core, a simple protocol realizing the \podtm notion with minimal interaction among parties and optimal latency, guaranteeing that transactions will be written and rounds will become past-perfect as fast as the network allows. Moreover, \podtm-core has accountability, guaranteeing that it is possible to identify (and potentially punish) parties who attempt to modify written transactions or vary their timestamps beyond the allowed limits. Based on \podtm-core, we construct \bidsettm-core, a protocol for agreeing on a unordered set of bids. Since \bidsettm-core inherits these properties, we obtain censorship resistant auctions.

\subsection{Our Techniques}
We consider that time proceeds in \emph{rounds} and that parties know the current round, so that we can express timestamps in terms of rounds. A \podtm associates each transaction $\tx$ with timestamp values $0 \leq \rmin$ (minimum round), $\rmax \leq \infty$ (maximum round) and $\rconf$ (\confirmed round). The confirmation round may be initially $\rconf=\perp$ but later becomes $\rmin \geq \rconf \geq \rmax$, when a transaction is \emph{confirmed}.  While each party reads different values $\rmin,\rmax,\rconf$ for the same $\tx$, \podtm guarantees that values read by different parties stay within certain limits.

When parties \emph{read} a \podtm, they obtain a $\podtm$ data structure $\Pod = (\txSet, \rperf, \ppcert)$, where $\txSet$ is set of  transactions with their associated timestamps, $\rperf$ is a \emph{past-perfect} round and $\ppcert$ is auxiliary data. The \emph{past-perfection} property of \podtm guarantees that, 
%if a transaction $\tx$ did not appear in $\txSet$, then no other party will ever read $\tx$ with confirmed round $\rconf'$ such that $\rconf' < \rperf$. This guarantees that, 
when a party reads a \podtm with $\rperf$, it gets all transactions that every other party will ever read with a confirmed round smaller than $\rperf$.  A \podtm also guarantees \emph{past-perfection within $w$}, meaning that $\rperf$ is at most $w$ rounds in the past. Moreover, \podtm guarantees \emph{confirmation bounds} ensuring that
% if a party reads $\tx$ with minimum round $\rmin$ and maximum round $\rmax$, for every other party who reads $\tx$ with $\rconf' \neq \perp$, it holds that $\rmin \leq \rconf' \leq \rmax$. This means that 
the minimum and maximum round values for $\tx$ read by a given party determine the range of confirmed round values for $\tx$ that will be read by every other party.

When a party writes a transaction $\tx$ to a \podtm, it obtains associated timestamps $\rmin, \rmax, \rconf$ and auxiliary data $\txcert$, which may later evolve. A \podtm guarantees \emph{confirmation with $u$} rounds: at $u$ rounds after $\tx$ was written, every party who reads the \podtm will see $\tx$ as confirmed with some $\rconf \neq \perp$. 

In summary, a \podtm provides \emph{past-perfection} and \emph{confirmation bounds} as safety properties, ensuring parties cannot be blindsided by transactions suddenly appearing as confirmed too far in the past and that the different (and continuously changing) transaction timestamps read by each party stay in a certain range. The liveness properties of \emph{confirmation within $u$} and \emph{past-perfection within $w$} ensure that new transactions must be confirmed within a bounded delay and that each party's past-perfect round must be constantly progressing.

Besides introducing the notion of \podtm, we present a construction \podtm-core that requires minimal interaction among parties and achieves optimal latency, \textit{i.e.}, optimal parameters $u=2\delta$ and $w=\delta$, where $\delta$ is the current network delay (not a delay upper bound). Our construction relies on a set of $n$ \emph{replicas} to maintain a \podtm data structure that can be read by an unbounded number of clients. The only communication is between each client and the replicas, not among clients nor among replicas. Moreover, \podtm-core can use the auxiliary data $\ppcert,\txcert$ to publicly identify misbehaving replicas and keep them accountable.

Writing a transaction $\tx$ in \podtm-core only requires clients to send $\tx$ to the replicas, who each assign a timestamp $\tsp$ (current time)  to $\tx$ and return a signature on $(\tx,\tsp)$. When reading the \podtm, the client simply requests each replica's database of  $(\tx,\tsp)$, validates the corresponding signatures and determines $\rmin$ and $\rmax$ as the minimal and maximum values of $\ts$ obtained as response, respectively. If the client receives responses from enough replicas, $\rconf$ is determined by taking the median of the $\tsp$ received from these replicas, ensuring that $\rmin \leq \rconf \leq \rmax$. If a replica provides conflicting timestamps $\tsp\neq \tsp'$ for the same $\tx$ to one or more clients, the replica's signatures publicly proves cheating.
