\section{Introduction}
Most decentralized systems implementing cryptocurrency schemes~\cite{bitcoin} and smart contract~\cite{smartcontracts,wood} have been built on the assumption that it is necessary to achieve consensus on \emph{total order} of all users'  transactions. This belief sparked a rich line of work on permissionless blockchain protocols~\cite{EC:GarKiaLeo15,C:KRDO17,EC:DGKR18,EC:PasShi18,algorand} and more traditional consensus protocols~\cite{CCS:MXCSS16,PODC:YMRGA19,SP:AMNRY20,sbft,narwhal} aimed at instantiating large scale high-throughput public ledgers that maintain an (eventually) immutable global order of transactions while quickly registering honest parties' transactions. However, as much as these protocols have evolved in recent years, the need to maintain total order among all transactions in the ledger introduces fundamental constraints to their efficiency~\cite{antoniadis_et_al:LIPIcs.DISC.2018.7,FOCS:BerGarPer89,10.1007/978-3-319-39028-4_9}. Moreover, the fact that leaders get to choose what data to register in the ledger allows these leaders to unfairly order transactions~\cite{C:KZGJ20} or even momentarily censor a transaction. While solutions to these censorship~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-10185} and order fairness problem have been suggested~\cite{CCS:KDLJK23,fairpos}, they face fundamental limits~\cite{EC:KiaLeoShe24} due achieving consensus on a total order of transactions.
\pagebreak

A recent line of work~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1909-10926, DBLP:conf/dsn/CollinsGKKMPPST20,DBLP:conf/aft/BaudetDS20,DBLP:journals/dc/GuerraouiKMPS22} established that consensus on a total order of transactions is actually not necessary for digital asset transfer schemes (\textit{e.g.} cryptocurrencies). The main insight in these works is that a ledger providing a \emph{partial order} of transactions is sufficient for implementing such schemes. More precisely, the ledger only needs to ensuring that, if transactions $\tx_1$ and $\tx_2$ are created by the same party, then every party outputs them in the same order. While this observation allows for constructing highly efficient protocols, previous works focus on the specific case of asset transfer and do not consider the censorship issue. Inspired by this line of work, we ask the following question:

\begin{center}
\emph{What are the weakest transaction ordering guarantees sufficient for building decentralized systems with optimal latency and censorship resistance?}
\end{center}

As a motivating example, we consider the case of distributed auctions where bids are registered on a public ledger~\cite{trustee,seal,ACNS:DavGenPou22,CCS:TAFWBM23,chitra_et_al:LIPIcs.AFT.2024.19,cryptoeprint:2024/1011}.  In this scenario, parties post their bids (either sealed or open) on the public bulletin board before a deadline, after which an auctioneer and/or the bidding parties execute a protocol to determine the winning bid (\textit{e.g.} select the highest bid). Notice that there is no need for the bids in a given auction to be ordered, as long as the parties agree on the set of bids corresponding to a given auction execution. Hence, in this application, even the weaker guarantee of partial order among transactions from the same user is an overkill. Moreover, if the leaders executing the public ledger act maliciously, they may be able to censor bids from certain parties.



\subsection{Our Contributions}
We answer the question above by introducing the notion of \podtm along with \podtm-core, a matching protocol. A \podtm allows for parties to read and write transactions that are associated with a \emph{timestamp}. Rather than agreeing on fixed timestamps for each transaction, each party in a \podtm sees different timestamps that may vary over time within certain limits.  This means that we may be able to order some transactions written at far enough points in time, while other transactions written around the same time may appear concurrent during the entire execution. Nevertheless, a \podtm has a  \emph{past-perfection} property, which guarantees that, after a certain round, no honest party will read a transaction with a timestamp earlier than that round. In terms of liveness, besides ensuring that new transactions will be written in a timely manner (preventing censorship), \podtm guarantees that every round will become \emph{past-perfect} within a bounded delay.  

We construct \podtm-core, a simple protocol realizing the \podtm notion with optimal latency, accountability guarantees and minimal communication among parties. This protocol guarantees that transactions will be written and rounds will become past-perfect as fast as the network allows. Moreover, it guarantees that it is possible to identify (and potentially punish) parties who attempt to censor new transactions or modify written transactions or vary their timestamps beyond the allowed limits. Based on \podtm-core, we construct a protocol for a agreeing on a set of bids that inherits these properties, giving us censorship resistant auctions.
