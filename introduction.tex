\section{Introduction}
Most distributed systems implementing cryptocurrency schemes~\cite{bitcoin} and smart contracts~\cite{smartcontracts,wood} have been built on the assumption that it is necessary to achieve consensus on \emph{total order} of all users' transactions. This belief sparked a rich line of work on permissionless blockchain protocols~\cite{EC:GarKiaLeo15,C:KRDO17,EC:DGKR18,EC:PasShi18,algorand} and more traditional consensus protocols~\cite{CCS:MXCSS16,PODC:YMRGA19,SP:AMNRY20,sbft,narwhal}, aimed at instantiating large scale high-throughput public ledgers that maintain an (eventually) immutable global order of transactions, while quickly registering honest parties' transactions. However, as much as these protocols have evolved in recent years, the need to maintain total order among all transactions in the ledger introduces fundamental constraints to their efficiency~\cite{antoniadis_et_al:LIPIcs.DISC.2018.7,FOCS:BerGarPer89,10.1007/978-3-319-39028-4_9}. Moreover, the fact that `leaders' get to choose what data to register in the ledger allows them to unfairly order and even briefly censor transactions~\cite{C:KZGJ20}. While solutions to censorship~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-10185} and order fairness~\cite{CCS:KDLJK23,fairpos} problems have been suggested, they face fundamental limits~\cite{EC:KiaLeoShe24} due to aiming for full consensus guarantees.
\pagebreak

A recent line of work~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-1909-10926, DBLP:conf/dsn/CollinsGKKMPPST20,DBLP:conf/aft/BaudetDS20,DBLP:journals/dc/GuerraouiKMPS22} establishes that consensus on a total order of transactions is actually not necessary for digital asset transfer schemes (\textit{e.g.,} cryptocurrencies). The main insight is that a ledger providing a \emph{partial order} of transactions is sufficient for implementing such schemes. More precisely, the ledger only needs to ensure that, if transactions $\tx_1$ and $\tx_2$ are created by the same party, then every party outputs them in the same order.

While this observation allows for highly efficient protocols, previous works focus on the specific case of asset transfer and do not handle censorship.
Moreover, previous partial-order protocols, being based on broadcast~\cite{DBLP:journals/iandc/Bracha87}, do not offer any notion of `totality' (what we later refer to as \emph{past-perfection}) of the transactions that exist in the system: parties may see $\tx_2$ after $\tx_1$, broadcast by the same sender, but they can not be sure whether a $\tx_3$ will appear after them, or \emph{when} this will happen. While sufficient for implementing payments, this is problematic for implementing protocols like auctions, where the set of all bids submitted \emph{up to a prescribed deadline} must be known.

In this work, we investigate how to further relax consensus guarantees in order to construct distributed systems with better liveness and censorship resistance. As a motivating example, we consider distributed auctions~\cite{trustee,seal,ACNS:DavGenPou22,CCS:TAFWBM23,chitra_et_al:LIPIcs.AFT.2024.19,cryptoeprint:2024/1011}, where bids are usually registered on a public ledger.  In such auctions, parties post their bids (either sealed or open) on the public ledger before a deadline, after which a protocol is executed to determine the winning bid (\textit{e.g.}, the highest bid). Notice that there is no need for the bids in an auction to be ordered, as long as parties agree on the set of bids corresponding to a given protocol execution. Hence, in this application, even the weaker guarantee of partial order among transactions from the same user is redundant, and the parties executing the public ledger can still censor bids. In this context, we ask the following question:

\begin{center}
	\emph{What are the weakest transaction-ordering guarantees sufficient for building distributed systems with optimal latency and censorship resistance?}
\end{center}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
We answer this question by introducing the notion of \podtm, which allows for parties to read and write transactions associated with a \emph{timestamp}. Rather than agreeing on a transaction's timestamp, each party in a \podtm reads different timestamps that may later change, but are guaranteed to stay within certain limits.  This means that we may be able to order some transactions that are written sufficiently far in time from each other, while transactions written around the same time may appear concurrent during the entire execution. A \podtm has a \emph{past-perfection} property, which guarantees that, for each round, there is a point in time by which an honest party will have read all transaction that may ever get associated with a timestamp earlier than that round. A \podtm also guarantees that written transactions can be read by all honest parties (preventing censorship) and every round becomes \emph{past-perfect} within a bounded delays.  

We construct \podtm-core, a simple protocol realizing the \podtm notion with minimal interaction among parties and optimal latency, guaranteeing that transactions become \emph{confirmed} and rounds become \emph{past-perfect} as fast as the network allows. Moreover, \podtm-core has accountability, guaranteeing identification (and enabling punishment) of parties that attempt to modify confirmed transactions, or vary their timestamps beyond the allowed limits. Based on \podtm-core, we construct \bidsettm-core, a protocol for agreeing on a unordered set of bids. Since \bidsettm-core inherits these properties, we obtain censorship resistant auctions.

These works propose protocols where it is only guaranteed that honest parties can order transactions produced by the same party, yielding more efficient protocols. However, besides being restricted to payments, these protocols do not address censorship issues and do not guarantee that honest parties will see transactions in a timely manner (\textit{i.e.} some honest transactions may only be seen after an unknown delay). \subsection{Our Techniques}
We consider that time proceeds in \emph{rounds}, and that parties know the current round, so we can express timestamps in terms of rounds. A \podtm associates each transaction $\tx$ with timestamp values $\rmin \geq 0$ (minimum round), $\rmax \leq \infty$ (maximum round) and $\rconf$ (\confirmed round). The confirmation round may be initially $\rconf=\perp$ but later becomes $\rmin \leq \rconf \leq \rmax$, when a transaction is \emph{confirmed}.  While each party reads different values $\rmin,\rmax,\rconf$ for the same $\tx$, \podtm guarantees that values read by different parties stay within these limits.

When parties \emph{read} a \podtm, they obtain a $\podtm$ data structure $\Pod = (\txSet, \rperf, \ppcert)$, where $\txSet$ is set of  transactions with their associated timestamps, $\rperf$ is a \emph{past-perfect} round and $\ppcert$ is auxiliary data. The \emph{past-perfection} safety property of \podtm guarantees that 
%if a transaction $\tx$ did not appear in $\txSet$, then no other party will ever read $\tx$ with confirmed round $\rconf'$ such that $\rconf' < \rperf$. This guarantees that, 
\txSet contains \emph{all} transactions that every other honest party will ever read with a confirmed round smaller than $\rperf$.  A \podtm also guarantees \emph{past-perfection within $w$}, meaning that $\rperf$ is at most $w$ rounds in the past.

For each transaction $\tx$ in \txSet, the reading party obtains associated timestamps $\rmin, \rmax, \rconf$ and auxiliary data $\txcert$, which may evolve. A \podtm guarantees \emph{confirmation within $u$ rounds}, meaning that, at most $u$ rounds after $\tx$ was written, every party who reads the \podtm will see $\tx$ as confirmed with some $\rconf \neq \perp$. 
Moreover, \podtm guarantees \emph{confirmation bounds}, ensuring that
% if a party reads $\tx$ with minimum round $\rmin$ and maximum round $\rmax$, for every other party who reads $\tx$ with $\rconf' \neq \perp$, it holds that $\rmin \leq \rconf' \leq \rmax$. This means that 
the values \rmin and \rmax for $\tx$, read by an honest party, determine the range of the \rconf value for $\tx$ that can ever be read by another honest party,
i.e., $\rmin \leq \rconf \leq \rmax$.

In summary, a \podtm provides \emph{past-perfection} and \emph{confirmation bounds} as safety properties, ensuring parties cannot be blindsided by transactions suddenly appearing as confirmed too far in the past, and that the different (and continuously changing) transaction timestamps stay in a certain range. The liveness properties of \emph{confirmation within $u$} and \emph{past-perfection within $w$} ensure that new transactions get confirmed within a bounded delay, and that each party's past-perfect round must be constantly progressing.

Besides introducing the notion of \podtm, we present a construction \podtm-core that requires minimal interaction among parties and achieves optimal latency, \textit{i.e.}, optimal parameters $u=2\delta$ and $w=\delta$, where $\delta$ is the current network delay (not a delay upper bound). Our construction relies on a set of $n$ \emph{replicas} to maintain a \podtm data structure, which can be read by an unbounded number of clients. The only communication is between each client and the replicas, not among clients nor among replicas. Moreover, \podtm-core can use the auxiliary data $\ppcert,\txcert$ to publicly identify misbehaving replicas and keep them accountable.

Writing a transaction $\tx$ in \podtm-core only requires clients to send $\tx$ to the replicas, who each assign a timestamp $\tsp$ (current time)  to $\tx$ and return a signature on $(\tx,\tsp)$. When reading the \podtm, the client simply requests each replica's log of $(\tx,\tsp)$, validates the responses, and determines $\rmin$ and $\rmax$ from the received timestamps. If the client receives responses from enough replicas, $\rconf$ is determined by taking the median of the timestamps received from these replicas.
