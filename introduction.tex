\section{Introduction}
Recent literature has recognized the redundancy of consensus for implementing simple asset-transfer systems,
given that users do not share accounts.
Such schemes have been described in theory and deployed in the real world.
Yet, their focus has been mainly on payment systems and not arbitrary distributed applications.

Censorship resistance is, on the other hand, an important feature of decentralized systems,
missing from many state-of-the-art and even deployed consensus protocols.
In leader-based protocols the leader arbitrarily selects the transactions to be included in the new block.
% In a model where at most $f$ of the $n$ replicas can be malicious, the adversary can delay honest transactions by $f$ blocks.
Similarly, in permissionless and dynamically-available protocols, such as Bitcoin and Ethereum,
the miner or the block builder have similar power.
Multiple works have proposed solutions.
One approach employs multiple leaders, which inevitably leads to duplication of transactions in the output of the protocol.
Other solutions employ cryptography, such threshold or time-based encryption, which sacrifice efficiency.


In this work we develop \emph{Partially Ordered Dataset (POD)},
a primitive that allows censorship-resistant recording of arbitrary messages (which we refer to as \emph{transactions}, as common in the literature).
POD can be used as the base for a wide range of consensus-less applications, such as payment systems,
auctions with non-omission guarantees, and storing feeds for decentralized social media.

POD associates transactions with \emph{timestamps}.
It does not guarantee full ordering, as classical consensus mechanisms do, but instead results in a `fluctuating order'.
That is, honest users may observe different timestamps for each transaction, yet the timestamps will be within a restricted and well-defined interval,
and users will agree on the set of observed transactions.
Moreover, POD uses the notion of a \emph{past-perfect} timestamp, akin to a `final' block in classical consensus algorithms.
When a timestamp becomes past-perfect, honest users are guaranteed that no more transactions will ever be output with
a timestamp smaller than the past-perfect.

By giving up on total order we result in a high-performant layer in terms of both throughput and latency.
The construction of POD is very simple. Replicas can run the code for Certificate Transparency to record transactions with timestamps,
with addition of a Merkle Mountain Range (MMR) data structure, which allows light clients to bloom-filter for inclusion efficiently.
Clients use the mean among the responses from 2/3 of the replicas.

Pod operates in a permissioned fashion among a committee (akin to Streamlet, HotStuff),
but we're exploring to make it semi-permissioned as well (proof-of-stake–capable, akin to Tendermint).
We provide resilience and some guarantees in the full Byzantine model, assuming an adversary that controls $f < n/3$ replicas,
and we are exploring ways to introduce rational arguments to the replicas.
Additional ordering guarantees are optional and provided in a `flexible', per-client manner, based on local beliefs and requirements.
Similar techniques have been used to resolve the dilemma between dynamically available and safe-under-asynchrony blockchains~\cite{DBLP:conf/sp/NeuTT21}.
We are exploring mechanisms to bring the rate of fluctuation down with time,
so that the system converges to full consensus, but the main guarantees are provided pre-consensus.
